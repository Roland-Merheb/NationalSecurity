================================================================================
2.  BACKGROUND AND OUR UNDERSTANDING
================================================================================

Nigeria's security agencies already hold a wealth of information and experience. The opportunity now is to move that information more quickly from collection to action, so that agencies in the field can act on it together.

The Office of the Special Adviser to the President on Homeland Security, created in May 2026, exists to close that gap through intelligence integration, inter-agency coordination, and proactive risk management. It builds on the Intelligence Fusion Centre established under the Office of the National Security Adviser, which already brings the major security agencies together in Abuja.

The next step is to extend that coordination to where threats occur: the land borders and the scene of a crisis. Two practical gaps stand out:

    a.  Unwatched borders. Long stretches of land border have no persistent
        surveillance, so illegal crossings go undetected.

    b.  Separate radio systems. Police, military and paramilitary units
        often operate on separate radio systems, which can make direct
        communication at an incident difficult.

Neither gap can be closed by the military alone. Nigeria's 2019 National Security Strategy defines national security broadly, reaching beyond military matters to economic, social, environmental and human-rights concerns. Its list of national threats includes porous borders, transnational organised crime, banditry and kidnapping, and public health challenges. Meeting them is shared work: the police, customs, immigration, civil defence, emergency services and communities each play a part alongside the armed forces. What SHIELD adds is earlier, clearer information where it is needed, at the border and at the scene: knowing who is moving, where, when and how, early enough to act before harm is done.

SHIELD addresses both with equipment, delivered and working, not with further studies.

3.  THE SHIELD CONCEPT
================================================================================

SHIELD is built on Nigeria's National Security Strategy 2019 (NSS), the overarching framework for national security. The NSS takes a comprehensive view of security centred on the well-being of people, recognises that security is no longer the exclusive preserve of the military, and calls for a coordinated, whole-of-nation approach. SHIELD equips civilian and military agencies to deliver on five of its commitments.

    Porous borders
        NSS:     Nigeria's land borders are largely porous and inadequately
                 manned; the South-West border with Benin is known for
                 smuggling; stronger border security is critical to
                 countering terrorism and organised crime
        SHIELD:  Package A - continuous monitoring of a pilot section of the
                 South-West border

    Transnational organised crime
        NSS:     Smuggling, the spread of small arms and trafficking are
                 fuelled in part by weak border security management
        SHIELD:  Package A - sensors, cameras and cargo scanning at border
                 posts

    Internal security
        NSS:     Commits to stronger border security and to
                 technology-enabled intelligence infrastructure
        SHIELD:  Package A - a joint border control room shared by all
                 border agencies

    Crisis response and resilience
        NSS:     Calls for early warning and response, effective
                 communication, and inter-agency crisis teams with unified
                 command, control, communication and coordination
        SHIELD:  Package B - radio bridging, a mobile incident command
                 vehicle and emergency communications

    Environmental threats
        NSS:     Treats floods and other natural and man-made disasters as
                 national security threats needing contingency plans
        SHIELD:  Package B - coordination and communications that keep
                 working when normal networks fail

SHIELD also supports the Secure and Prepare work streams of the National Counter-Terrorism Strategy (NACTEST), on which the NSS relies for its counter-terrorism aims.

Two principles guide the design:

    a.  Shared picture, not shared command. Each agency keeps its mandate and
        chain of command. SHIELD gives them the means to see and talk to each
        other.

    b.  Build on what exists. SHIELD extends the existing coordination
        structure rather than creating a new one.

4.  PACKAGE A - BORDER MONITORING AND MANAGEMENT
================================================================================

Package A gives the agencies that manage Nigeria's land borders a clear, continuous view of what crosses them. The main users are the Nigeria Customs Service, the Nigeria Immigration Service, the Police and the Nigeria Security and Civil Defence Corps, working alongside the military where needed.

The aim is not only to intercept armed threats. It is also to keep border communities safe in their daily lives, curb smuggling and human trafficking, and let legitimate trade and travellers move more safely and quickly.

The stakes are high. The National Security Strategy, citing the Small Arms Survey, estimates that some one million small arms and light weapons are in circulation in Nigeria, and links their spread to conflicts in neighbouring countries and weak border security management.

The pilot covers a section of the South-West border with Benin, one of Nigeria's busiest trade corridors, and expands to other sections once proven. The exact section, for example along the main road corridors and the bush crossings between them, will be fixed during the site survey.

4.1  How it works

    1.  Detect: radar and ground sensors pick up people and vehicles
        crossing away from official posts, day and night.

    2.  Verify: the nearest camera turns toward the alert, so an operator
        can see what it is before anyone is sent, which avoids wasted
        patrols and false alarms.

    3.  Share: video and alerts reach the control room, where all border
        agencies work from the same picture, and on to national level.

    4.  Respond: the agency best placed to deal with the situation acts,
        whether a customs team, an immigration officer, a police unit, or a
        military patrol for armed threats.

4.2  Equipment

    Monitoring towers
        Masts with day, night and thermal cameras and detection radar,
        covering open and bush terrain.

    Ground sensors
        Buried sensors along known unofficial crossing routes that alert
        when someone passes.

    Border post upgrades
        CCTV, vehicle barriers and gates, and space, power and network ready
        for ID devices.

    Joint border control room
        A room near the border where staff from all border agencies monitor
        cameras and sensors together.

    Tower power
        Solar panels with battery storage, independent of the grid.

    Communications links
        Radio and satellite links carrying video and alerts to the control
        room and onward.

    Mobile monitoring units
        Vehicles with a camera and radar mast, moved to where crossing
        patterns shift.

    Portable observation kits
        Portable day and night cameras carried by border officers to cover
        gaps between towers.

    Vehicle and cargo scanning
        X-ray scanners so customs can inspect vehicles and cargo for
        contraband without unloading, speeding up legitimate trade.

    Site protection
        Fencing and controlled entry around border posts and the control
        room.

5.  PACKAGE B - JOINT EMERGENCY RESPONSE AND COMMUNICATIONS
================================================================================

Package B helps every agency that responds to an emergency work as one team at the scene. That includes the police, civil defence, fire service, the National Emergency Management Agency (NEMA) and state emergency agencies, the Federal Road Safety Corps, health services and, where needed, the military.

Emergencies include not only attacks and kidnappings but also floods, building collapses, chemical spills and other hazardous-material incidents, major road accidents, fires and large public events.

In June 2026, NEMA and the World Bank began reviewing Nigeria's disaster management frameworks, with the aim of strengthening coordination between agencies, clarifying roles and closing gaps in response structures. Package B supports that effort with the equipment that lets agencies work as one team at the scene.

5.1  How it works

At any major incident, responders arrive on their own radio networks and cannot talk to each other. The radio bridging equipment links those networks, so the incident commander can open a shared channel for all responders in minutes, without anyone changing radios.

The mobile incident command vehicle becomes the on-scene coordination point. Agency leads plan together inside it, with a satellite link to national level. If phone networks are down, as often happens in floods or large incidents, the emergency communication kits keep the scene connected.

5.2  Equipment

    Radio bridging equipment
        Gateways linking the radio systems of different agencies, fixed at
        coordination centres and portable for the field.

    Mobile incident command vehicle
        One equipped vehicle with radios, bridging equipment, satellite
        link, displays and a meeting space for agency leads.

    Emergency communication kits
        Satellite phones and portable satellite terminals for when phone and
        internet networks fail.

    Deployable coordination shelters
        Quick-erect shelters with lighting and power for longer operations
        or places a vehicle cannot reach.

    Standby power
        Generators and battery packs for the vehicle, shelters and kits.

    Mobile relay masts
        Portable masts with secure radio relay, extending coverage into
        remote areas.

6.  DESIGN PRINCIPLES
================================================================================

All SHIELD equipment is chosen against six principles:

    a.  Technology-neutral: no brand is fixed in advance; equipment is
        selected against performance requirements.

    b.  Open standards: video, data, and radio interfaces use recognised
        standards, so SHIELD connects to national systems and avoids lock-in
        to a single supplier.

    c.  Modular and scalable: the pilot section and first vehicle form a
        template that can be copied to other border sections and zones.

    d.  Built for the environment: equipment is rated for heat, humidity,
        heavy rain, and dust.

    e.  Independent power: towers and field sites run on solar and batteries;
        command equipment carries its own backup.

    f.  Protected against tampering and cyber attack: links are encrypted,
        equipment is physically hardened, and access is controlled.

7.  IMPLEMENTATION APPROACH
================================================================================

SHIELD is delivered in steps, starting with an early live demonstration of radio bridging with participating agencies. Timings for each step will be confirmed in the final proposal, after the site survey.

    Step 0 - Site survey and design
        Survey the pilot border section and coordination sites; confirm the

    Step 1 - Launch and demonstration
        Equipment ordered and export licenses applied for; live demonstration
        of radio bridging with participating agencies.

    Step 2 - Interoperable communications
        Radio bridging equipment and emergency communication kits delivered,
        installed, and handed over.

    Step 3 - Incident command vehicle and border pilot
        Mobile incident command vehicle delivered; pilot border section
        installed and operational.

    Step 4 - Expansion
        Further border sections and response units, based on pilot results.
